Electricity-Sector Consequential Methods

What the public consultation record actually says — every claim linked to the panel that shows it

In mid-2025 the GHG Protocol's electricity-sector working track put a draft framework for consequential emissions-impact methods out for public comment: a formula for quantifying the grid impact of an intervention, nine candidate additionality tests, three families of marginal emission-rate methodology, and the weighting between them. This site is the consultation record, restructured so it can be read: a documented dataset, ten pages of panels, and the assessment below. A consultation is not a referendum, and at these bases it is not a survey either. What follows is a third-party reading of a self-selected record — no more, and no less.

1 · What this consultation is, and who showed up

The consultation ran under the GHG Protocol's electricity-sector work on consequential methods — quantifying what an intervention changes on the grid, as distinct from the attributional inventory accounting of Scope 2. Its twenty-page document put a Scope 2 technical-working-group subgroup formula, an additionality battery, marginal emission-rate method families and a build/operating weighting question out for comment.

185 respondents filed; 180 form the analytical base. Four junk submissions (gibberish or single-letter answers, shown verbatim on the integrity page) and one superseded resubmission are excluded from every figure on this site. The room is 57.8% US (104 of 180) and company-heavy (60 of 180), with 159 filing as organizations and 21 as individuals; 41.7% (75 of 180) asked for their identity to be withheld. Attrition is steep and nearly monotone: the first substantive question drew 160 answers, the last 34 — by the consultation's own evidence question, four-fifths of the room had left. Every base on every panel matters for exactly this reason.

One caveat governs everything below: a consultation is not a referendum, and a self-selected 180 is not a survey of any population. The record shows who chose to speak and what they argued — it cannot show what the field believes.

2 · The headline: the proposed formula does not command consent

Asked directly whether the proposed formula is appropriate, 62.8% said No — 81 of the 129 who answered. The more useful reading is why, and the coded themes on the explain question cover 80 of the 81 No respondents. The No side is not three even verdicts; it is one dominant argument with two smaller companions, and the three overlap — they are emphases, not a partition. The dominant argument is incomplete impact accounting (50 of 80): the formula counts avoided emissions without netting induced ones, and it is the single most side-separating code in the taxonomy — 62.5% of coded No respondents carry it against 5 of the 39 coded Yes respondents. Second, wrong venue or wrong sequence (35 of 80): integrate this with Scope 2 recognition (19), coordinate the workstreams first (18 — a code no Yes respondent carries), keep the inventory/impact boundary clean (10). Third, burden, data and equity (18 of 80): complexity and audit burden (14), attribution uncertainty (8), regional equity (6), data availability (2). The Yes side is near-monolithic by comparison: 33 of its 39 coded respondents simply call the structure sound.

Support concentrates where the method would be used commercially: among self-declared energy suppliers/utilities the split runs 7 Yes to 5 No (n=12), and among data/analytics providers 3 to 2 (n=5) — bases small enough that both are color, not findings. Redacted respondents lean further No than named ones (41 of 60 against 40 of 69), but the lean does not clear significance and is best treated as direction only (§7).

3 · Genuine splits, not consensus

Away from the formula, the record divides. Whether to quantify secondary effects splits the room 56 to 50 (n=106) — and it splits again by identity, with named respondents at 35 Yes / 21 No and redacted ones at 21 / 29, one of only two redaction contrasts in the set that clears significance. Temporal granularity is a dead heat: Annual 31, Hourly 30, Sub-hourly 14, Monthly 3 (n=78) — binned, fine 44 against coarse 34. Spatial granularity splits four ways across the whole coarse-to-fine ladder (Country 21, Nodal 20, Grid region 18, Balancing area 15, Zonal 3; n=77). Against all of that, annual rather than lifetime reporting, at 80.8% (80 of 99), looks settled — but it is settled mainly among US respondents, 96.4% of whom (53 of 55) chose annual, against 61.4% (27 of 44) everywhere else.

That pattern generalizes: country is the sharpest axis in this dataset, and it is not the redaction axis. US respondents reject the formula harder than the rest of the room (73.0% No, 54 of 74, against 49.1%, 27 of 55), reject regional differentiation of additionality (24.6% Yes, 14 of 57, against 56.1%, 23 of 41) and want finer temporal granularity (69.0%, 29 of 42, against 41.7%, 15 of 36). Japan is the mirror image on both headline calls — 6 of its 7 respondents called the formula appropriate, and all 6 who answered chose annual — though at n≤8 Japan and the UK are read as sketches, not estimates. Every one of these splits is visible when the Decision Board is segmented by country; the significance tests this assessment leans on — here and in §7 — come from the project's verification audit of the underlying dataset, not from any shipped panel — the methodology page records the distinction.

4 · The additionality gradient is coherent

Rating nine candidate additionality tests required, optional or not required could have produced noise. It produced a clean three-tier gradient: a required tier (regulatory at net +47.2, 66R/21O/17N on n=104; timing at +25.5, n=102), an optional middle (positive list, financial analysis, contractual/tenor — all modal-Optional, within 1.5 points of each other and not meaningfully ranked), and a rejected tier ending at first-of-its-kind (−46.9, 8R/35O/53N on n=96). The gradient is coherent with the separate feasibility question: the tests the room wants required are the tests it thinks can actually be administered (regulatory 78 and timing 71 picks of n=95), and the verification pass measured the two rankings as near-identical (rank correlation 0.90, top three places the same). This is a crowd answering "what works", not venting.

The tensions are real but specific: 10 of 95 respondents said no test is feasible; whether additionality should vary by region is unresolved three ways (37 yes / 32 unsure / 29 no, n=98); whether rigor should vary by claim type splits 42 to 33 (n=75). And the widest could-versus-must gap is the positive list: 61.1% call it feasible while 15.7% would require it, a 45-point spread that marks it as the room's preferred optional instrument.

5 · The methodology scoreboards, read with humility

Each method family was asked as two multi-selects — which are appropriate, which are not — answered by different rooms of 54 to 66 people, so every figure here is a difference of shares on two bases, printed on the axis. With that humility: each family has clear leaders. Operating margin: SCED-locational (net +52.7) and statistical methods (+48.2), with capacity-factor-based (−28.6) and difference-based (−24.6) rejected. Build margin: recent capacity additions (+54.8) far ahead, average emission rate (−43.3) far behind. Weighting: the GHGP Grid-connected Guidelines (+30.5) lead a thin field. The verification pass found the leaders robust — all three stay first, and strengthen, when circulated template text is collapsed to a single voice.

The middle is genuinely unsettled, and one option's verdict depends on the dedup: build-margin capacity-expansion modeling nets −3.4 as filed, and the same verification pass measured it flipping to +5.5 once template blocs are collapsed, so no honest sentence calls it accepted or rejected. Equally telling are the non-answers: on weighting, "unsure" drew 14 picks on the appropriate side and 20 on the not side, "none are appropriate" 9, "all are feasible" 15. No method in any family commands a majority even of this small, self-selected base. The field, asked to pick its tools, largely answered "not yet".

6 · The evidence base is thin, and the record is rhetorical

The consultation asked for supporting research and documentation. 34 respondents answered, five with a bare "N/A" — 29 substantive evidence submissions against 180 respondents and 1,329 free-text answers. Citation mining across all that text is the better evidence map: 72 distinct cited domains, with citation rates led by NGOs (8 of 21, 38.1%) and trailed by consultants (5 of 25, 20.0%). Most of the record argues rather than demonstrates.

Some of it argues in unison. Circulated template text moves headline numbers: deduplication trims the formula question from 48 Yes / 81 No to 47 / 71, and costs the secondary-effects No side 9 of its 50. The quote browser badges template passages so an argued position can be told from a propagated one, and a 4,000-character export cap means the longest answers are read slightly truncated. None of this is misconduct — it is the normal texture of an open consultation, and the reason raw counts are never the whole story.

7 · Interest, coordination and integrity — symmetric skepticism

Read every position against who benefits from it. Granular, model-heavy methods create work for modeling consultancies and data vendors, several of whom filed; simple, coarse methods flatter reporters with weak interventions to defend, and companies are the largest single block in the room (60 of 180). Both interests are real; neither invalidates an argument, and this site declares neither side right.

Coordination is visible and bounded: eight template blocs, the largest a five-member pack whose members are all redacted and — per the verification audit — all from a single non-US country: a national coordination effort, not an industry one, and deliberately not named here. The second-largest, a four-member bullet-formatted pack, has one named member (SEMI). Engie Impact filed twice through different individuals; both filings count, grouped as a family.

Redaction correlates with the conservative position — but only twice significantly in seven stance questions: on secondary effects, and most sharply on regional differentiation, where redacted respondents pick "unsure/depends" 22 of 41 against 10 of 57 named. On the headline formula question the lean is direction, not finding. And the axis is confounded: companies are 41 of the 75 redacted respondents and the only org type where redaction is the majority, so every "redacted respondents think X" is substantially "companies think X". A handful of filers appear in both this consultation and the Scope 2 consultation; their persistence is context, not extra weight.

8 · How a third party should read this record

Signal is strongest where volume, consistency and reasoning line up: the formula as proposed lacks consent (and the modal objection — net the induced against the avoided — is a design instruction, not a rejection of consequential accounting); regulatory and timing tests belong in any additionality battery; annual reporting is preferred, emphatically so in the US; and an average emission rate is not an acceptable build margin. Genuinely open, on this record: secondary effects, spatial and temporal granularity, most of the methodology middle, and regional differentiation — where the split runs along national lines the room may not have expected.

What would settle the open questions is exactly what the record lacks: published back-tests of the candidate methods, registry pilots, the evidence a 29-submission base barely surfaced. Until then, this consultation can legitimately inform three things — where the draft's design consensus already exists, where a standard must offer options rather than mandates, and which claims the eventual framework will be asked to police. It cannot decide the contested middle, and it does not pretend to.
