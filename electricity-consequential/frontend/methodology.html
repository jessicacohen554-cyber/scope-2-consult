What this page is. Every number on this site comes out of one deterministic pipeline that starts at a single committed spreadsheet. This page documents that pipeline: where the data came from, how questions and respondents are identified, which five submissions are excluded and on what evidence, the arithmetic rules that govern every chart, how the free text was coded, what is masked for privacy, what the record cannot support, and the exact commands to reproduce all of it. The companion dataset README documents the tables — schema, columns, views, SQL recipes. This page documents the judgments. Where they overlap, the README is the reference and this page links to it rather than restating it.

Provenance & build chain

One input file, committed unmodified, reshaped by a deterministic script chain. Nothing on this site is hand-entered.

Consultation document

The spreadsheet is committed at the hub root and never edited. Everything under data/ is regenerated from it; if a figure on this site is wrong, the fix belongs in a script, not in a cell.

The build chain

Stage 1 — build
scripts/build_dataset.py
Reads the workbook, detects each column's shape, joins the hand-curated codebook, and writes six CSVs, the SQLite database and manifest.json. Mechanical only.
Stage 2 — validate
scripts/validate_dataset.py
86 checks: cell-conservation round-trip against the workbook, selection-explosion conservation, label coverage, anchor and parent integrity, and the frozen dataset figures. Non-zero exit on any failure.
Stage 3 — derive
scripts/analytics/derive_flags.py
Per-respondent integrity flags into data/derived/: junk candidates, resubmissions, entity families, shared-text clusters and blocs, citations and domains. Emits candidates; decides nothing.
Stage 4 — curate
reference/ — audit, exclusions, themes, quotes
The human layer: the organization audit and the adjudicated exclusion list, the theme taxonomy and every free-text answer coded against it, and the curated quote set. Each has its own test suite.
Stage 5 — export
scripts/analytics/export_frontend.py
Recomputes every aggregate on the analytical base, applies the privacy mask, and writes the frontend/data/*.json contract this site reads. A companion validator re-derives every exported number independently from the database.

Mechanical and editorial are separated on purpose

Three files carry the build, and the split between them is the point: a reader who disagrees with a judgment can find it in one place, change it, and rebuild. No interpretation is buried in the reshaping code.

build_dataset.py

Mechanical. Header parsing, shape detection, whitespace normalization, multi-select explosion, table writing. Knows nothing about what any answer means.

survey_meta.py

Editorial, structural. Section groupings, the stringency ladder, the granularity ladders, the special-option list, the polarity map, per-question caveats, and the closed vocabularies its load_labels() gatekeeper enforces.

question_labels.csv

Editorial, per question. 56 hand-judged rows: shorthand, label, topic, document section, concern category, answer shape, and a notes column disclosing every wording defect found.

The build fails loudly rather than guessing: a missing label row, a repeated or numeric shorthand, an unrecognized vocabulary value, a subcategory filed under the wrong category, an empty hazard note, or a leftover tbd placeholder each stop the build and name the offending question.

Question identity

Stable join keys that survive the export's numbering gaps, its sub-numbered matrix, and one malformed header.

question_id
The immutable join key. Zero-padded so it sorts correctly: Q003…Q052 for ordinary questions, Q026_1…Q026_9 for the nine additionality-matrix rows.
question_number / sub_number
The integer parts — Q026_4 is number 26, sub-number 4.
display
The human-facing form used in every chart label and popover on this site: Q19, Q26.4. Never used as a key.
shorthand
A unique snake_case slug carrying what the question is about — formula_appropriate, addl_regulatory_req, temporal_granularity_max — so queries read as English. Unique across all 56 columns and forbidden to contain digits, both enforced at build time.
The header parser tolerates a malformed row

Question 26 arrives as nine sub-numbered headers, "26.1."–"26.9.". One is defective in the source: 26.6 Posititve list — missing its second period and misspelling "Positive". The parser ^\s*(\d{1,3})(?:\.(\d))?\.?\s makes the trailing period optional, so 26.6 parses as 26.6 regardless.

The export is not corrected. The typo is disclosed in the codebook's notes column and reproduced here; silently fixing source text is how provenance dies.

Numbering has gaps at both ends

Survey numbers 1, 2, 7 and 8 are absent from the export; numbering runs 3–52. Questions 3–17 are the respondent profile (13 columns), 18–52 the substantive record (43 columns, including the nine matrix rows).

respondent_id runs 3 to 201 with gaps and is the survey tool's own identifier, not a row index. Never assume 1…185, and never renumber: the IDs are how a claim on this site is traced back to a submission.

The analytical base — 185 raw, 180 analytical

Four junk submissions and one superseded resubmission are excluded from every aggregate, every quote and every browser on this site. They are deleted from nothing.

What the base is

Raw base
185 submissions — every row in the export. data/respondents.csv keeps all 185, and the integrity page shows the excluded ones in full.
Analytical base
180 = 185 − 4 junk (IDs 11, 12, 14, 31) − 1 superseded (ID 100). Every percentage, chart, theme count and quote on this site divides by this base or by a question's own subset of it.
Named vs redacted
Of the 180: 105 named, 75 redacted (41.7%). Three of the five excluded submissions were named, so the named-organization browser covers 108 − 3 = 105 profiles.

The junk rule, as implemented

An answer counts as gibberish when it is shorter than 20 characters and trips at least one of three tests. A respondent becomes a junk candidate when at least 50% of their free-text answers are gibberish. The detector is content-only: it never looks at a name, an organization, a country or a closed-question answer, which is what lets it adjudicate redacted submissions without inferring identity.

Repeated single character

After stripping non-alphanumerics, one distinct character remains. Catches e, aaaa, 1111.

Vowel ratio below 0.30

Vowels ÷ letters. Catches consonant mash such as gfnhgf, zdfb, znzfn.

Keyboard run ≥ 3

Three or more keys adjacent on one QWERTY row, in sequence, either direction. Catches asdf, qwerty.

The thresholds are choices, and they are disclosed. 0.30 is the implemented vowel ratio; a laxer 0.35 would still catch all four submissions, but 0.30 combined with the keyboard-run test reproduces the per-respondent gibberish tallies exactly, so 0.30 is what ships. The rule is deliberately a candidate detector, and no submission is excluded on its output alone.

The resubmission rule: keep the later filing

Where the same non-placeholder name appears on two submissions, the later, fuller filing counts and the earlier one is excluded and disclosed. Here that is 100 → 151. This is not a duplicate-merge: nothing from ID 100 is folded into ID 151, and the answers that changed between the two filings are shown side by side on the integrity page rather than quietly overwritten.

An entity family is not a duplicate. Engie Impact appears twice (IDs 135 and 160) — different named individuals, different answers. Both count toward the base; they are grouped as a family for display only.

The adjudication trail

Detection, decision and enforcement are three separate steps carried by three separate artifacts, so that no single automated pass can quietly drop a submission from the record.

Detect. derive_flags.py sweeps all 185 submissions and writes candidates to data/derived/respondent_flags.csv. It sets no exclusion. 4 junk + 1 superseded
Adjudicate. A human pass reviews each candidate's text and records a reason and a written evidence string in reference/exclusions.csv — the authoritative list. 5 rows
Enforce. The exporter reads exclusions.csv and filters those respondents out of every aggregate, quote pool and organization file it writes. 180 in scope
Analytical base 180 of 185

The full 185-row sweep found a fourth, ID 31: a redacted submission whose six free-text answers are all runs of the letter a, with the matrix straight-lined "Optional" — the same signature as ID 12, and 100% gibberish against the ≥50% rule. Constructs & arithmetic

Five rules govern every percentage on this site. Each exists because the obvious shortcut produces a specific, nameable misreading.

Rule 1 — the additionality matrix is stringency, not sentiment

Questions 26.1–26.9 ask whether each of nine additionality tests should be Required, Optional or Not required. That is an ordered scale of how binding a test should be — carried as answer_numeric 3 / 2 / 1 with construct = stringency.

"Required" is not approval and "Not required" is not disapproval. A respondent who thinks the regulatory test is sound but should stay optional is not expressing half-support. So the matrix is painted with a sequential ramp, never the green/red support ramp, and it is never averaged against anything else. Support colors on this site are reserved for questions that carry an explicit polarity declaration.

The one derived figure the matrix carries is net requiredness = %Required − %Not required, on each test's own base. It is a sort order, not a score.

Rule 2 — polarity is declared, proposal-relative, and sometimes absent

A binary Yes/No means nothing until you know what the consultation document proposed. Polarity is declared once, in survey_meta.POLARITY, and validated at build time against the actual answer options:

Twelve picklist options are escape hatches rather than positions. They are flagged is_special, carry no rank, render as their own neutral-gray segments, and stay out of every net figure:

"All are feasible" on Q49 means the opposite of a pick. Q49 asks which weighting approaches are not feasible to implement. Selecting "All are feasible" states that nothing belongs on that list. Counting it as one more not-feasible selection inverts its meaning exactly. This is the concrete reason the no-netting rule exists.

Rule 4 — scoreboard nets use each question's own base

The three methodology scoreboards each pair two independent multi-select questions with different denominators: operating margin Q35 (appropriate) against Q36 (not appropriate), build margin Q38 against Q39, weighting Q47 against Q49. Respondents did not have to answer both, so the two lists are not complements.

net % = (share of the appropriate base) − (share of the not-appropriate base), and both bases are always printed beside the bar. Worked example — SCED – locational, the operating-margin front-runner:

Q35 "appropriate" — 47 of the 66 analytical-base respondents who answered Q35 71.2%
Q36 "not appropriate" — 10 of the 54 analytical-base respondents who answered Q36 − 18.5%
net, analytical base +52.7

On the raw base the same option reads 48 / 69 = 69.6% against 12 / 57 = 21.1%, for a net of +48.5 — the figure the planning digest carried. The gap between +48.5 and +52.7 is entirely the three junk submissions that ticked boxes on both questions. It is a small number that changes no conclusion, and it is exactly the kind of drift that goes unnoticed when a base is left unstated.

A methodology absent from both lists is unknown, not neutral — nobody is counted as indifferent by default. And the weighting pair is asymmetric on top of everything above: Q47 asks which approaches are appropriate, Q49 which are not feasible to implement. An approach can be appropriate and infeasible at once, so Q49 is not Q47's inverse and its net is read with more caution than the other two.

Rule 5 — the granularity ladders are ceilings, and the binning is stated

Q43 and Q45 ask for the maximum appropriate spatial and temporal granularity — a ceiling, not a preference. Answering "Nodal" is not a demand for nodal data; it is a statement that nodal would not be too fine. Both are ranked coarse → fine:

Theme coding

This survey has no reason picklists. The free-text record is the record — so it was read, in full, against a closed vocabulary fixed before the reading began.

The taxonomy

43 theme keys, plus an uncodeable sentinel, each documented with a definition and inclusion rules in reference/theme_taxonomy.md. Keys were drafted from a reconnaissance pass over eight questions spanning every topic, then locked before the full coding pass so the vocabulary could not be quietly grown to fit whatever turned up. Each key carries one of four polarities:

concern

Names a problem or risk of consequential accounting or of the proposal — attribution_uncertainty, complexity_burden, gaming_greenwashing_risk.

support

Argues the value of consequential accounting or of a proposed element — net_impact_completeness, corrects_attributional_blindspots.

design

Prescribes how the standard should be built — tiered_rigor_by_claim, standardization_guidance, annual_truing_up.

neutral

Contextual or informational content — evidence_pointer, workstream_coordination.

Polarity belongs to the theme, not to the respondent. Coding an answer complexity_burden says the answer argues that complexity is a problem; it says nothing about whether that respondent supports the proposal overall. Theme bars on this site are never read as approval ratings.

The coding pass

Scope
1,335 free-text answers coded across 20 substantive questions — every substantive free-text answer in the survey except those of junk IDs 11/12/14 and superseded ID 100, which were skipped before the reading began. ID 31's six answers were coded before its junk ruling landed; the exporter filters them, leaving 1,329 coded answers on the analytical base.
Multi-label
An answer gets every theme it argues. 3,300 code assignments over 1,335 answers — a mean of 2.47 themes per answer, ranging from one (439 answers) to thirteen. A long submission arguing five distinct objections is coded with five keys.
Coverage
94.0% raw — 80 of 1,335 answers are uncodeable (an "N/A", a dash, a bare cross-reference, no thematic content). Counting each distinct normalized text once, coverage is 97.8% dedup-adjusted: the uncoded residue is disproportionately short boilerplate that repeats.
Template propagation
Answers whose normalized text is identical carry identical codes — the text is read and coded once, then propagated. This makes coordinated submissions visible as coordination rather than as independent agreement.

Dedup-adjusted counts

Every theme count on this site is published twice: a raw count of answers, and n_dedup, which counts each shared-text cluster's members once. When a five-member response pack files the same paragraph, the raw count says five and the dedup count says one. Neither is wrong; presenting only the first would be.

The clusters that drive the adjustment are found mechanically, not editorially. A shared-text cluster is a normalized string of ≥200 characters appearing verbatim in ≥2 submissions; this build finds 38 clusters spanning 26 respondents. A bloc is a connected component over respondents sharing ≥2 distinct clusters — and that two-cluster threshold matters: a ≥3 threshold dissolves the five-member policy-insights pack entirely. Eight blocs emerge, of which two are substantial: a five-member pack (IDs 43, 47, 60, 79, 82 — all redacted) and a four-member bullet-formatted pack (IDs 51, 86, 89, 117) spanning Q18–Q44.

Where human judgment enters — and where it stops. Three places, and they are the ones to argue with. (1) The taxonomy: which 43 keys exist, and where the boundaries between them fall. Four seed keys were merged and one — workstream_coordination — was added mid-pass when more than ten respondents independently argued a position no key covered; both changes are disclosed in the taxonomy document. (2) Applying keys to answers: one reader, no second coder, and therefore no inter-rater agreement statistic to quote. (3) Quote selection — 65 quotes across five topics, verbatim substrings capped at 600 characters with […] elisions, tested for verbatim fidelity against the database. Two guards constrain all three: a theme is coded only when the answer argues it, never when it merely names a concept in passing; and codes come from the text alone — never from the respondent's org type, country, or closed-question stance. Full definitions and inclusion rules are in reference/theme_taxonomy.md.

Privacy & masking

Two in five respondents asked not to be identified. At n=180, honoring that requires coarsening the segments, not just hiding the names.

Four coarse dimensions, and no fifth

Cross-tabulation is how anonymity leaks: a respondent who is the only Financial Institution in Japan filing as an individual is identifiable without ever being named. So this site exposes exactly four segment dimensions, each deliberately coarse, and offers no way to combine them. Counts are on the analytical base of 180:

DimensionValues (analytical base, n=180)

country_4 us 104 · uk 12 · jp 8 · other 56 (24 countries collapsed; only three clear the threshold alone)

responding_as organization 159 · individual 21

The source data has 13 organization types, 28 countries and 24 sectors. Those live in the dataset and are documented in the README; they are not offered as chart filters, because at this sample size several of their cells contain one person.

The n<5 sentinel

Any segment cell covering fewer than five respondents is emitted as the literal sentinel {"n": "<5"} — the underlying counts are never written into the exported JSON at all, so they cannot be recovered from the page source.

Renderers hatch those cells. A masked cell is never printed as a number and never drawn as zero: "too few to show" and "nobody" are different facts, and conflating them would invent a finding. Masked cells still appear in the visually-hidden data table beside each chart, labelled as masked.

Redaction handling

77 of 185 respondents (41.6%) requested redaction; 75 of the 180 on the analytical base (41.7%). Their name and organization fields read "Redacted" — never null, and never blank. A field that is empty but not redacted reads "Not provided", so the two cases stay distinguishable at a glance.

Redacted respondents are never named, anywhere, on any page. They are counted in every aggregate; they appear in quotes only as "Redacted — Company, US"; they are excluded from the named-organization browser, which is why it covers 105 profiles and not 180; and in bloc membership lists they appear as a count, never as an ID paired with a name. The derived tables carry no name or organization column at all — a property the flags test suite asserts on every run.

Redaction is not an accusation. Redacted respondents lean measurably against the proposed formula, against secondary effects, and toward coarser granularity — a real and reportable pattern. It is not evidence of bad faith. There are ordinary reasons to file without attribution, and a headline built only on attributable responses skews the other way. Both directions of that skew are shown on the integrity page.

Limitations

What this record is not. Every item here constrains a reading someone will otherwise be tempted to make.

1. The sample is self-selected

Nobody was sampled. 185 organizations and individuals chose to respond to a technical consultation on consequential electricity accounting. That selects for parties with a stake, the expertise to engage, and the staff time to write — and against everyone affected by the outcome who has never heard of it. Percentages describe these respondents and no wider population.

2. It is US-heavy

104 of 180 (57.8%) are United States respondents; the UK is next at 12 and Japan at 8. Any global reading of this record is substantially a reading of the US market's view. Where a geographic split is reportable it is shown with its n — and those ns are frequently single digits.

3. Attrition is steep and monotone

On the analytical base, response counts fall 160 → 129 → 106 → 99 → 66 → 34 from Q18 to Q52. The later questions are answered by a persistent minority who are not a random subset of the earlier one. Cross-question comparisons in the back half of the survey compare different rooms of people, and this site prints both ns whenever it makes one.

4. The evidence base is thin

Q52 — the consultation's own request for supporting research and documentation — drew 36 answers on the raw base (34 analytical), several of them "N/A". Citation mining across the whole free-text record finds 138 citation-bearing answers from 49 respondents across 74 linked domains. The record is argued far more than it is evidenced, and a position's popularity here carries no information about its empirical support.

5. Some submissions are coordinated

38 shared-text clusters span 26 respondents, including a five-member and a four-member response pack. Identical passages inflate naive counts and propagate identical citations. Every theme count on this site carries a dedup-adjusted companion, and quotes drawn from shared text are badged — but coordination is not misconduct, and a well-organized coalition is entitled to file.

6. Long answers are truncated

The survey tool cut answers at 4,000 characters. 18 substantive answers sit at that cliff and are flagged likely_truncated. Those are the most detailed submissions in the record — so the truncation is not random, and where a quote ends abruptly the tool, not the respondent, may have ended it.

A consultation is not a referendum
Counting responses is not counting votes
A consultation collects arguments so a standard can be improved. The count of respondents taking a position is a fact about who showed up, not a mandate — and at n=180, self-selected, it is not a survey of the field either. Where this site reports "63% said No", the honest reading is "of the 129 analytical-base respondents who answered this question, 81 said No", which is what the chart actually prints.

What this site can support

That specific positions were argued, by whom, with what stated reasoning, and in what proportion among these respondents. That some questions produced a clear majority and others a genuine split. That certain concerns recur across unrelated submissions. That the additionality tests fall into a coherent gradient. Which claims were evidenced and which asserted. And — because the exclusions, blocs and citation flags are published rather than applied silently — how much of any given count survives dedup.

What it cannot

That any position represents the field, the industry, or "stakeholders" in general. That a method rejected here is technically unsound, or one favored here is sound — popularity among 66 respondents is not evidence. That subgroup differences are real where the ns are small; most are not tested and many cells are masked. That redaction implies motive. That the consultation's outcome should follow the counts. And nothing at all about parties who did not respond.
