What would a “GHG impact statement” actually measure?

1 · Attributional vs consequential

Two questions, two statements

An inventory asks what sits inside your boundary. An impact statement asks what changed in the world because you acted.

The draft multi-statement framework keeps these two questions apart. Statements 1 and 2 are inventories built with attributional accounting. Statement 3 is built with consequential accounting. AMI White Paper (RFI, Mar 2026) p.5

Attributional accounting
“Attributional accounting (also known as inventory accounting): A type of GHG accounting that quantifies total GHG emissions (and removals and other accounting categories, if applicable) within a defined inventory boundary and time period.”
AMI White Paper glossary · AMI White Paper pp.17–18
Consequential accounting
“Consequential accounting: A type of GHG accounting that estimates the impacts or changes in GHG emissions (and removals) resulting from specific projects, actions, or interventions relative to a baseline scenario in which the project, action, or intervention did not occur. It quantifies the systemwide or global change in GHGs in the atmosphere caused by an action, project, or intervention.”
AMI White Paper glossary · AMI White Paper p.18

The electricity consultation draws the same line and adds the key consequence for boundaries: the impact view ignores whose inventory the change lands in. Consequential electricity consultation (Oct 2025) p.5

“The project-based accounting approach evaluates system-wide emissions impacts of the project or intervention in question, without regard to the reporting company’s operational or organizational inventory boundary.”

Public Consultation – Consequential Electricity-Sector Emissions Impacts, p.5 · source

Four statements are under consideration

Statement 1Physical GHG inventoryAttributional. “existing, no change”. Holds scope 2 location-based.
Statement 2Market-based GHG inventoryAttributional. Holds scope 2 market-based.
Statement 3GHG impact statementConsequential. Impacts of actions against a baseline.
Statement 4Non-GHG indicatorsKey performance indicators.

Numbering and the phrase “existing, no change” from the Explanatory Memo; scope 2 placement from White Paper Figure 6. AMI White Paper pp.4–5, Fig. 6 p.36 AMI RFI Explanatory Memo p.4

Statement 3 · GHG impact statement
“The GHG impact statement provides a dedicated, structured statement for reporting on the impacts of actions taken by the reporting company inside and outside its value chain (e.g., emissions avoided, reduced, or removed). It applies to actions such as projects, interventions, investments, production/sale of products, and purchase/consumption of products. This statement uses consequential accounting methods that aim to quantify the global change in GHGs in the atmosphere resulting from a given action. This involves estimating the GHG impacts of an action relative to a credible baseline scenario in which the action did not occur.”
AMI Phase 1 White Paper, RFI version · AMI White Paper p.6

The name is a draft

White Paper Figure 6 lists alternative names: “GHG impacts/outcomes of actions”, “GHG outcome statement” and “Mitigation intervention statement”. AMI White Paper Fig. 6, p.36

“Names of statements reflect the current majority view of the AMI TWG. They are draft versions and subject to change.”

Figure 6, note 3 · AMI White Paper p.36

Not a footprint

“The GHG impact statement does not provide a comprehensive assessment of a corporate footprint, but instead allows reporters to report the impact of activities that are quantified using consequential methods and therefore not reflected in the physical GHG inventory.”

2 · Direction of travel

Additionality is proposed as a Statement 3 requirement

The TWG’s draft text uses “shall”. How to test it is still open, and so is whether one action can appear in two statements.

Additionality (one of eight optional definitions listed)
“A criterion often applied to GHG projects stipulating that project-based GHG reductions should only be quantified if the project activity ‘would not have happened anyway’—i.e., that the project activity (or the same technologies or practices it employs) would not have been implemented in its baseline scenario—and/or that project activity emissions are lower than baseline emissions.”
AMI White Paper p.21, option 1, citing the Project Protocol · AMI White Paper p.21

The electricity consultation frames it more briefly: “the principle that claimed emission reductions or avoided emissions must result from actions that would not have occurred otherwise.” Consequential electricity consultation p.9

“Statement 3: Actions and market instruments shall be additional, i.e. reflect emissions reduction, avoidance, or removal that would not have occurred in the absence of the reporting company’s intervention (Note: specific additionality tests to be determined).”

Draft text, AMI TWG in-person workshop, Day 3 (2 Jul 2026), PDF p.18 · source
Sub-bullets for credits and other actions; the expected quality criteria

The same slide adds sub-bullets: for credits, a reference to ICVCM; for other actions, “Companies shall demonstrate that emissions reductions or removals from projects meet additionality requirements based on established frameworks or standards. (SBTi)”. AMI workshop Day 3 deck, PDF p.18

Can one action appear in more than one statement?

“Decisions have also not been made on whether statements are mutually exclusive or not.” The 9 September 2026 deck set out three options. AMI White Paper p.35 AMI TWG 2.08 deck, PDF p.27

Option 1 · may, if different

“Companies may account and report changes in GHG emissions [and removals, if applicable] resulting from a single activity through Statement 2 and Statement 3 as long as the quantified impacts of the activity are different and there is no double counting of impacts across the two statements.”

Option 2 · shall not

“Companies shall not account and report changes in GHG emissions [and removals, if applicable] from a single action in both Statement 2 and Statement 3. …”

Option 3 · S2 triggers S3

“Companies may account and report [emissions/removals or changes in emissions/removals] resulting from a single activity through Statement 2 or Statement 3. However, if the organization reports changes in GHG emissions [and removals, if applicable] in Statement 2 then, it shall also quantify and report in Statement 3.”

Option text verbatim from AMI TWG 2.08 deck (9 Sep 2026), PDF p.27

“Statement #1, statement #2, and statement #3 provide different perspectives of the same organization and therefore should not be seen as additive. Thus, the results from each statement shall be kept separate and shall not be added or subtracted from each other.”

Proposed ISO text, same slide · AMI TWG 2.08 deck, PDF p.27

The minutes record that, for ISO WG4, “the working group concluded that the statements are not mutually exclusive”. The July workshop had posed the question for Statement 1 vs Statement 3: “Should that be required, optional, or prohibited?” Its boiler example answered one case: “Is it ok to report both? - yes”. AMI TWG 2.08 minutes p.4 AMI workshop Day 3 deck, PDF pp.10, 14

Gap

No decision on mutual exclusivity has been published. For a company whose own action cuts its scope 1, it is not settled whether that cut may also be quantified in Statement 3, or how a Statement 2 claim and a Statement 3 claim on the same activity would be kept from double counting. AMI White Paper p.35

3 · Existing GHG Protocol tools

Building blocks GHG Protocol already has

Statement 3 is new. The methods are not. Baselines, primary and secondary effects, and grid margins have been in force since 2005 and 2007.

Baseline scenario (Project Protocol)
“The baseline scenario is a reference case for the project activity. It is a hypothetical description of what would have most likely occurred in the absence of any considerations about climate change mitigation.”
Three generic possibilities: “implementation of the same technologies or practices used in the project activity”; “implementation of a baseline candidate”; or “the continuation of current activities, technologies, or practices…” · Project Protocol (2005) §2.8, p.12
Primary and secondary effects (Project Protocol)
“A primary effect is the intended change caused by a project activity in GHG emissions, removals, or storage associated with a GHG source or sink. … A secondary effect is an unintended change caused by a project activity … Secondary effects are typically small relative to a project activity’s primary effect. In some cases, however, they may undermine or negate the primary effect.”
Secondary effects include “One-time effects” (construction, installation, decommissioning) and “Upstream and downstream effects” (recurring, from inputs and products). · Project Protocol §2.4, pp.11–12
Operating margin and build margin (Grid Guidelines)
“Generation displaced from existing power plants is referred to as the ‘operating margin’ (OM). Generation from potential new capacity, whose construction is avoided due to the project activity, is referred to as the ‘build margin’ (BM).”
The baseline rate combines them: “ER_baseline = wBM + (1-w)OM” (Eq.1, p.13). · Grid Guidelines (2007) §2.3, p.11
Grid Guidelines §12.2.2 (as quoted in the consultation, p.7) Primary Effect_t = Baseline Emissions_p,t – Project Activity Emissions_p,t
Secondary Effect_s,t = Baseline Emissions_s,t – Project Activity Emissions_s,t
GHG Reduction_t = Primary Effect_t + Σ_s (Secondary Effect_s,t)

Grid Guidelines §12.2.2, p.71 Consequential electricity consultation p.7 The project term is a rate times the project’s generation, so a project with non-zero emissions carries them here.

Mapping the effects of one action

The next figure applies these blocks to one action that the next page studies: adding carbon capture to a power plant. No GHG Protocol document draws this map; this site's research notes assemble it from the pieces above.

STEP 1 · PRIMARY, AT THE PLANT

The rate cut is a plant-level effect

For a retrofit that lowers an existing process’s emission rate, the Project Protocol baseline is “the historical GHG emission rate for the process or equipment that is being retrofitted”. Project Protocol Box 8.7, p.58 The Grid Guidelines treat a reduction “at the coal-fired power plant itself” as not grid-connected. Grid Guidelines Table 2.1, p.12

STEP 2 · PRIMARY, ON THE GRID

Changed output moves other plants

Box 8.2 gives retrofits a second primary effect: “displacing GHG emissions from other sources, for any amount of production greater than historical levels, due to increased utilization.” Project Protocol Box 8.2, p.52 Those MWh are valued at a weighted mix of operating and build margin. Grid Guidelines Eq.1, p.13

Inference

Capture usually lowers net output, so by symmetry the lost MWh are an emissions increase met by other generators.

STEP 3 · SECONDARY, ONE-TIME

Construction and decommissioning

“One-time effects—Changes in GHG emissions associated with the construction, installation, and establishment or the decommissioning and termination of the project activity.” Project Protocol §2.4, p.11

Inference

Applied to capture: capture units, pipelines and storage wells.

STEP 4 · SECONDARY, UPSTREAM

Fuel and methane

Upstream examples include “changes in the release of methane (CH4) during coal mining” and “CH4 leaks from gas transport and storage”. Project Protocol §5.3.2, p.32 For grid projects the Guidelines say fuel-chain changes “can often be ignored”, but should be included “if their magnitude is more than a few percent of the expected primary effect”. Grid Guidelines §4.3–4.5, pp.27–29

STEP 5 · SECONDARY, DOWNSTREAM

Transport, injection, EOR

“Companies shall account for all life cycle GHG emissions that occur throughout the geologic storage pathway (i.e., cradle to grave).” With enhanced oil and gas recovery this extends to the produced hydrocarbons’ use. LSR Ch.14 Req. 19.GS, p.344

STEP 6 · MARKET AND LIFETIME

Market responses and life extension

“Market responses occur when alternative providers or users of an input or product react to a change in market supply or demand caused by the project activity.” Project Protocol §5.3.2, p.32 Chapter 16 methods “can be applied to quantify the impacts of EOR production, which would include the displacement of non-EOR oil and gas production.” LSR Ch.14 p.357

Inference

If capture keeps a plant running that would otherwise retire, those extra years compare against the replacement mix, not the unabated plant (Box 8.2 end-of-life barrier).

Where the results go: outside the inventory

“To evaluate the impacts of specific actions, companies should use project or intervention accounting methods, which estimate the systemwide GHG impacts of an action relative to a counterfactual baseline scenario in which the action did not occur”

LSR v1.0 Ch.16, p.398 · source

“Any estimates of avoided emissions must not be included in or deducted from the company’s scope 1, 2, or 3 inventory.”

LSR v1.0 Ch.16, p.409 · source
More in-force context: LSR Ch.16 disclosure, Scope 2 Guidance, Policy and Action Standard, Corporate Standard Ch.8

Chapter 16 also sets disclosure duties for anyone who reports impacts separately: they “shall disclose the data sources, methods, and assumptions used to quantify the impact(s) of the evaluated action(s), the assessment boundary, the assessment time period, whether it is an ex ante and/or ex post assessment, and whether the results have been third-party verified.” It leaves the location to the AMI workstream, which “will define where to report the impacts of actions in a GHG report”. LSR Ch.16 pp.397, 411 (endnote 1)

The 2015 Scope 2 Guidance already keeps avoided emissions outside the scopes. Scope 2 Guidance (2015) §4.2, p.28

“This guidance does not support an ‘avoided emissions’ approach for scope 2 accounting due to several important distinctions between corporate accounting and project-level accounting. However, companies can report avoided grid emissions from energy generation projects separately from the scopes using a project-level accounting methodology.”

Scope 2 Guidance (2015) §4.2, p.28 · source

Policy and Action Standard

Its glossary separates “Intended effects: Effects that are intentional based on the original objectives of the policy or action.” from unintended ones, which “may include a variety of effects, such as rebound effects, lack of compliance or enforcement …”. Its baseline is “A reference case that represents the events or conditions most likely to occur in the absence of the policy or action (or package of policies or actions) being assessed.” Policy and Action Standard (2014) glossary pp.175–180

Terminology note: its Table 6.3 lists “Carbon capture and storage” as a sink category (“Removal and storage of CO2”). LSR v1.0 Ch.14 (2026) says captured fossil CO2 “is not accounted for and reported as a removal”. P&A Table 6.3, p.54 LSR Ch.14 p.343

Corporate Standard Ch.8 precedent

“When companies implement internal projects that reduce GHGs from their operations, the resulting reductions are usually captured in their inventory’s boundaries. These reductions need not be reported separately unless they are sold, traded externally, or otherwise used as an offset or credit.” Its CHP exception: reductions “at the plants where this electricity would have otherwise been produced will not be captured in the inventory of the company installing the on-site plant.” AMI White Paper Annex C, pp.48–49

Inference

Changes at your own plant show in your inventory. Changes at other plants on the grid do not, and belong in separate reporting.

Signal of direction · 30 Jun–2 Jul 2026

The TWG listed four candidate baseline types, two from the Project Protocol and two from the Policy and Action Standard: “Project-specific procedure (Project Protocol)”, “Performance or industry benchmark baseline (Project Protocol)”, “Scenario method (Policy and Action)”, “Comparison group method (Policy and Action)”. The Day 2 deck records “Four baseline types proposed agreed”. AMI workshop Day 1 deck, PDF p.50 AMI workshop Day 2 deck, PDF p.5

4 · Consultation, Oct 2025 – Jan 2026

The electricity consequential consultation

The Scope 2 TWG subgroup proposed a simple formula and nine additionality tests. Most respondents said the formula was not appropriate.

Subgroup formula (§6.1) Emissions Impactt = Σi∈P_y (MWhi × MERi)
Hourly form Emissions Impact = Σt=18760 (MWhtprocured × MERt)

“P_y = set of all procurement transactions in the reporting year; MWh_i = megawatt hours procured in transaction i; MER_i = marginal emission rate applicable to the grid region, time, and conditions of transaction i”. Consequential electricity consultation §6.1, pp.7–8

  • One year at a time. The approach “recommends limiting the analysis to the reporting year period only and suggests reporting these impacts each year.” Consultation p.8
  • No secondary effects. It “does not consider secondary effects of electricity projects and focuses quantification entirely on the primary effects related to changes on the grid within a given reporting year.” Consultation p.9
  • Clean procured power. The draft notes “For renewable energy generation the project scenario Primary Effect emissions are usually zero”. Consultation p.7
Inference

The formula has no project-emissions term. It treats procured generation as zero-emitting, so it cannot carry a plant’s residual emissions, a lost-output penalty or fuel-chain effects. The Guidelines form (baseline minus project, plus secondary effects) can. The AMI TWG’s July 2026 boundary proposal, “Include all significant GHG effects of the action in the GHG assessment boundary”, also points away from omitting secondary effects. Gap

The consultation never mentions retrofits, uprates, repowering, CCS or capture; existing resources enter only through the margin definitions. The time basis is also unsettled: the TWG proposes annual ex-post, most respondents favour annual, while the Guidelines default to 10 years. No source says how lifetime effects would be shown on an annual basis. Consultation pp.13–15 AMI workshop Day 2, PDF p.67 Grid Guidelines p.71

5 · Closing

What to take into a position

What is decided, what is reasoning, and what is still open.

Takeaway

Statement 3 would measure the change in the atmosphere caused by an action against a baseline, reported apart from the inventories and, in the draft, not netted against them. The tools for it already exist in the Project Protocol and Grid Guidelines. On the research’s reading (inference), the electricity consultation’s simpler formula drops the parts that matter for any plant that still emits.

  • SOURCED (revision, not adopted). Draft text says Statement 3 actions “shall be additional”, with tests “to be determined”; the Secretariat said on 9 Sep 2026 that additionality “must be present in Statement 3”. AMI workshop Day 3, PDF p.18 AMI TWG 2.08 minutes p.6
  • SOURCED. 63% of 133 respondents said the subgroup formula was not appropriate; the regulatory test was the most often “required” (64%), first-of-its-kind the most often “not required” (54%). Feedback summary pp.5, 7
  • INFERENCE. For actions on emitting plants, the Grid Guidelines structure (baseline minus project emissions, plus secondary effects) is the GHG Protocol method that fits; the subgroup formula would need a project-emissions term restored.
  • GAP. Whether the same change can sit in Statement 1 and Statement 3, or in Statements 2 and 3, is undecided; three options are on the table. AMI TWG 2.08 deck, PDF p.27
  • GAP. Which additionality tests will apply to Statement 3, and how a retrofit or life extension would be tested, has not been addressed.
