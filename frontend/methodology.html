Methodology

Where the data comes from, what every number means, how each derived field is defined — and the limits of what any of it can show. With the exact commands to rebuild the whole thing from one spreadsheet.

The source file

Everything on this site derives from a single published file: S2-PublicConsultationFeedback-RawData-2026.07.29.xlsx, the GHG Protocol's raw export of its Scope 2 public consultation, published 2026-07-29. It is one sheet, clean_data_final1, 1,072 rows × 181 columns. That workbook is committed unmodified at the repository root and is the only input — every file under data/ is regenerated from it by scripts/build_dataset.py.

The reshape is mechanical and verified: every non-empty source cell appears exactly once in responses_long with its value unchanged (70,089 cells, 0 mismatches), and sum(n_selected) equals the response_selections row count (52,552). Nothing is aggregated away and nothing is discarded — including the text respondents typed into "Other" boxes, which is kept and flagged rather than folded into the option list.

Two files carry the judgement calls, and they are the only place to argue with an interpretation: scripts/survey_meta.py (section groupings, scale constructs and anchors, ordinal ladders, off-scale labels, closed vocabularies) and reference/question_labels.csv (the hand-curated shorthand, label and grouping for each of the 180 questions). The build fails loudly, naming the question, if a row is missing, a shorthand repeats, a vocabulary value is unrecognised, or method contradicts category.

The build chain

1
S2-…-RawData-2026.07.29.xlsx → scripts/build_dataset.py

Mechanical reshape, with survey_meta.py and reference/question_labels.csv supplying the editorial layer.

2
→ data/scope2_consultation.sqlite · data/*.csv · data/manifest.json

Tidy tables: questions, responses, response_selections, question_options, respondents, plus nine analysis views. CSV and Excel mirrors of the same content.

3
→ scripts/analytics/derive_flags.py → data/derived/

Coordination, citation and evidence-basis flags: respondent_flags.csv, text_clusters.csv, vector_clusters.csv, citations.csv, domains.csv. Definitions in section 4.

4
→ scripts/analytics/export_frontend.py → frontend/data/*.json

Reads the database, the derived tables, and two hand-curated inputs — reference/org_audit.csv (the classification audit) and reference/curated_quotes.json — and emits the nine contract files plus one profile per named respondent.

5
→ validate_dataset.py (27 checks) · validate_frontend_data.py (96 checks)

Both must pass before anything renders. The frontend validator re-derives hard numbers straight from the database and refuses to agree with the exporter on trust: every likert cell must sum to its own n, every segment must sum to the overall total, and every curated quote must be a verbatim substring of the respondent's stored answer.

Nothing under data/ is ever hand-edited. The pipeline only adds: data/derived/, the reference/ curation inputs, and frontend/data/. If a number here looks wrong, the fix is a script change and a rebuild, never a spreadsheet edit.

2. How the questions are classified

Four deliberately orthogonal axes, so a question can be found from whichever direction you come at it.

The consultation's own structure is a linear list of 180 questions. That is a poor index: the cost of hourly matching, the reasons for opposing it and the stance on it all sit in different places, and a reader who wants "everything about one proposal" or "every cost question" has to know the question numbers by heart. The codebook (data/questions.csv) therefore carries four cross-cutting axes. None is a sub-division of another — that is the point, and it is why a single question carries all four.

method 3 values

Which accounting method is at stake? mbm (116 questions), lbm (48), general (16). The one predicate that selects a whole method.

Example method = 'lbm' selects all 48 location-based-method questions. category = 'lbm_design' finds only 29 of them, because the cost, hourly-data, readiness and usefulness questions moved to the cross-cutting categories — which is the whole reason both axes exist.

What does the question interrogate? Cost? Data availability? Decision-usefulness? Cuts across both methods and all 27 sections.

Example category = 'cost_and_burden' returns 19 questions — and it collects them regardless of which proposal they hang off. Q78 (internal effort for hourly matching) and Q139 (procurement price for hourly-matched, deliverable certificates) sit together there while belonging to different policy levers.

The 27 sections are our grouping, not the GHG Protocol's. The raw export has no section column. The 27 topic sections were derived by reading the questions in order, and the consultation document's own numbering — Q159 refers to "section 5.3.1" in its wording — is not reproduced in the export, so there is no way to recover it. Do not cite a section number on this site as if it were a clause of the consultation document. role, anchor_question and parent_question are likewise derived from question wording, using rules in build_dataset.py. To regroup, edit SECTIONS in scripts/survey_meta.py and rebuild. The vocabularies for method, category and asks_for are closed and rejected at build time if violated; policy_lever and the section list are editorial.

3. What the 1–5 scores mean

The survey reuses a bare 1–5 box for at least seven different constructs. A 5 is not always good news.

Never pool scale scores across constructs. Averaging Q71 (support for hourly matching, 5 = strongly supports) with Q78 (internal effort, 5 = much more effort than today) produces a number that means nothing at all: one 5 is an endorsement and the other is a complaint. Filter on scale_construct first, every time. The heatmap on this site groups its rows by construct and prints a direction badge on each group for exactly this reason, and the exporter's validator fails the build if a single question ever mixes constructs.

Off-scale answers are shown, never folded in. Options such as "Unsure", "Unknown", "No basis to assess", "Insufficient basis to assess" and "Prefer not to say" carry answer_numeric = NULL and is_off_scale = 1 in question_options. In the exported cells they live in a separate off count that never enters c, the mean, or any support/oppose share. Where the off-scale count is large it is the finding — 44% of respondents answered "Unknown" on the residual-mix lead-time questions, and treating that as a middling score would erase it.

4. Derived fields, defined precisely

Nothing on this site is derived by eye. Each definition below is the rule the script actually implements, with the count it produces on the shipped build.

Text normalization — the recipe everything keys on
Lowercase → collapse every run of whitespace to a single space → strip → SHA-1. Nothing else: no punctuation stripping, no stemming, no fuzzy matching. Two answers are "the same" only if they are byte-identical after that transform. This matters more than it sounds, because the source arrived littered with roughly 1,900 non-breaking, narrow-no-break, thin, ideographic and zero-width spaces from respondents pasting out of Word — build_dataset.py normalises those upstream, which is both what stops otherwise-identical option labels splitting into separate strings and what lets duplicate detection see through the paste artefacts.
The same recipe is used by the quote curation, so cluster hashes computed in either place line up.
scripts/analytics/derive_flags.py → normalize_text()
Text cluster
A normalized string of at least 200 characters shared by at least 3 distinct respondents. A respondent who posts the same string to several questions counts once. Each cluster records its member ids, the question numbers it appeared under, the named/redacted split, a 240-character preview, and whether the text itself carries a citation signal.
On the shipped build: 323 clusters covering 177 respondents; the most-shared single string appears under 19 respondents. The 200-character floor is what keeps stock one-liners ("We disagree with proposed changes…") out of the cluster table while still catching them in the raw counts.
data/derived/text_clusters.csv · thresholds MIN_CLUSTER_CHARS = 200, MIN_CLUSTER_MEMBERS = 3
Template flag (is_template_response)
Set when a respondent shares clustered text with at least 3 other respondents — co-sharers counted across all of that respondent's clusters and deduplicated by respondent, so posting three copies of one shared text does not qualify you. Result: 155 of 1,072 respondents (14.5%).
The threshold is a documented judgement call, and the plan it implements is self-inconsistent. PLAN.md §3.5 describes the population as "shares ≥1 verbatim ≥200-character answer with ≥2 others" and reports ~154 respondents; §6 specifies a "≥3 clustered strings" rule. On the shipped data those two readings give 177 and 106 respondents respectively — neither lands on the ~154 the digest reports. The implemented rule keeps §6's threshold of 3 but applies it to co-sharers rather than strings, which reproduces the digest (155). n_shared_texts carries the raw count of distinct clustered strings per respondent, so anyone who prefers a different cut can compute it without re-running the pipeline.
Consequence for every statistic on this site: a headline of the form "N respondents said X" is inflated wherever X is template text. The integrity page recomputes the Q71 opposition share with and without dedup and shows the delta rather than picking one.
data/derived/respondent_flags.csv · MIN_TEMPLATE_CO_SHARERS = 3
Vector cluster
A respondent's scale vector is the sorted tuple of (question_number, answer_numeric) over every scale question they answered — the 20 questions in v_scale_answers, i.e. all likert_1_5 plus scale_labeled. Off-scale picks are absent by construction, since they carry a NULL score. Respondents whose full vectors are byte-identical and at least 10 items long form a cluster.
"Full" is the strict part: two respondents who agree on ten questions but differ on an eleventh are not clustered. That makes a match hard to produce by coincidence and is why an identical 20-item vector is treated as evidence of shared drafting rather than shared opinion. On the shipped build: 18 clusters, the longest identical vector running 20 items.
data/derived/vector_clusters.csv · MIN_VECTOR_LENGTH = 10

Entity families
A curated regular-expression map over the free-text organization field, grouping filings that came from one corporate group or institution. Nine families on the shipped build: luxshare (12), avary (5), engie (3), edf (2), rio_tinto (2), deloitte (2), edinburgh (2), mit (2), action_speaks_louder (2).
Two details make the difference between a family that works and one that quietly misses half its members. Luxshare filed under both Latin and Chinese trading names, so the pattern matches luxshare|立讯 and resolves 12 respondents where the digest's Latin-script-only count was 9. And MIT is matched case-sensitively, because a lowercase "mit" is the German preposition and would otherwise capture every German-language answer.
This is a patch over uncontrolled free text, not a resolution of it — see the limitation on organisation names in section 5. Its purpose is the narrow one of showing that 1,072 respondents are not 1,072 independent entities.
scripts/analytics/derive_flags.py → ENTITY_FAMILIES · names from PLAN.md §3.6
Citation signals
Eight regular expressions run over every free-text answer: url, doi, etal, peer_review, pdf, preprint (arxiv / ssrn / zenodo / biorxiv), journal, paren_year. citations.csv records an answer matching any signal: 812 answers from 258 respondents.
has_citation is deliberately stricter, counting only the seven hard signals and excluding paren_year — a bare "(2024)" in a sentence is not a citation. That gives 675 answers from 203 respondents (18.9% of all 1,072). URLs alone: 613 answers from 172 respondents.
Two things a citation signal is not. It is not a claim about quality: the survey never asks whether a source is peer-reviewed, so a link to a journal article and a link to a blog post both register as url. And it is not independent evidence when it travels inside template text — duplicated long answers carry citations too, so any citation count must be deduplicated on the normalized-text hash before it is reported.
Named respondents carry citation signals at 28.3% (141 of 499) against 10.8% for redacted respondents (62 of 573) — a 2.6× gap that runs the same direction as the redaction gradient in section 5, and one more reason a named-only read of this consultation looks different from the whole.
data/derived/citations.csv · SIGNAL_PATTERNS, HARD_SIGNALS
Domain classes
Every matched URL is reduced to its registrable domain — a small public-suffix list keeps bbc.co.uk from collapsing to co.uk — then classified against a curated map held in the script. Unmapped domains fall to other. On the shipped build, 206 distinct domains:
  • peer_reviewed (11 domains) — journal and publisher hosts: sciencedirect, cell.com, nature, PNAS, doi.org, Springer, Wiley, IOP and similar.
  • peer_reviewed_or_preprint (3) — SSRN, arXiv, Zenodo. Kept as a separate class rather than merged into the one above, because a preprint has not been peer-reviewed and calling it so would overstate the evidence base. These three are heavily used: SSRN alone appears in 97 answers.
  • government (32) — .gov, europa.eu, IEA, NREL, LBNL, national regulators.
  • advocacy (13) — energytag.org, resource-solutions.org, theclimategroup.org, cebuyers.org, zerogrid.org and similar campaign or standard-promotion sites.
  • data_vendor (4) · standard_setter (2, ghgprotocol.org) · shortener (2).
  • other (139) — everything unmapped.
Two honest limits. Shorteners are unverifiable: tinyurl.com (16 answers, 9 respondents) and bit.ly (1) hide their targets, and nothing in this pipeline resolves them — a shortened link is counted as a citation signal but its destination is unknown. And 139 of 206 domains are unclassified. The class map covers the domains that appear often enough to matter for the aggregate picture; the long tail is genuinely uncategorised, so class shares should be read as shares of classified traffic, not of all links.
data/derived/domains.csv · DOMAIN_CLASS, CLASS_BY_SUFFIX
Evidence-basis flags
Booleans read straight from respondents' own answers, not inferred: evidence_empirical, evidence_operational and evidence_general_only come from the Q138 multi-select (is_canonical = 1 selections only), and readiness_basis from the Q122 answer text. These are self-reports about the basis of a respondent's own view — which is what makes them useful and also what caps what they can support.
The Q158 exemption-evidence free text is a separate, hand-coded layer: all 297 answers were read and coded into data/derived/q158_evidence_coding.csv against nine codes, multiple codes per answer allowed. Frequencies: feasibility_argument 222, cost_argument 163, data_access_argument 148, sme_equity_argument 122, no_evidence_assertion 67, other 35, anecdote_only 16, cites_study 16, cites_own_data 9.

Evidence hierarchy for the basis string (capped at 200 characters, and it must say which rung it used): the legal form in the organisation's own name (GmbH / LLC / Ltd / SA → company; "Association" / "Council" / "Federation" → trade association) → the respondent's own *_other write-ins, which frequently state it outright → their free-text self-descriptions elsewhere in the survey → common knowledge of well-known bodies. Redacted respondents are never audited — there is nothing to audit, so they are unverifiable by construction rather than by finding. When claimed and audited agree, the row is still emitted, so the "no change" cases are visible alongside the reclassifications.

Quote curation criteria
106 quotations across nine policy levers, 4–6 per lever per side (fewer where a side is genuinely thin), selected against four criteria applied in order:
  • Substantive — argues a mechanism, cites evidence, or gives a concrete operational detail. Boilerplate agreement is excluded however representative it is.
  • Diverse — spread across organisation types, geographies, and named as well as redacted respondents.
  • Not template-duplicated — checked against the same normalized-hash duplicate map described above. Where a template text is a bloc's canonical argument and belongs in the record, it appears exactly once and carries its cluster hash as a visible badge. 13 of the 106 quotes are badged this way.
  • Quotable at ≤600 characters — a clean verbatim substring, never a paraphrase, with elisions marked […].
Attribution is "Organisation — Org type, Country" for named respondents and "Redacted — Org type, Country" otherwise. A redacted respondent is never named and never guessed at, even where their text hints at their identity. The verbatim property is not taken on trust: validate_frontend_data.py asserts that every non-elided segment of every quote is a substring of that respondent's stored answer to that question, and fails the build otherwise.
The curation is balanced by construction — both sides' strongest available material, not a case. Where a side's best material is template text, that is reported rather than padded around with weaker singletons.

Privacy and noise guard
The exporter never emits a breakdown for a segment value with fewer than 5 respondents overall. Those cells carry an {"n": "<5"} sentinel instead of counts, and the heatmap renders them hatched with no value printed — never as a zero, which would read as a finding. This is both a privacy measure on a dataset where half the respondents asked not to be identified, and a noise measure: a mean over three answers is not a segment result.

5. Known limitations

What this dataset cannot tell you, stated as plainly as what it can.

A consultation is not a poll

The 1,072 respondents are self-selected from an unbounded population. There is no sampling frame, no weights, and no way to estimate who did not respond. Nothing here supports a statement about what "the market" or "stakeholders" believe.

Response counts measure mobilisation — who turned up and who organised — not truth and not prevalence. That cuts in every direction: 155 respondents share substantial template text, and the coordinated groups on both sides of the hourly-matching question are visible in the data. A proposal opposed 70 : 22 in this consultation has been opposed by the people who filed, which is a different claim from being wrong.

Only 342 respondents answered all seven MBM stance anchors, though 978 answered at least one. Anchor-to-anchor comparisons are therefore not a fixed panel: the population shifts between questions, and part of any difference between two anchors is a difference in who chose to answer.

Redaction bias: named-only reads skew supportive

573 of 1,072 respondents (53.5%) requested redaction, so only 499 responses are attributable. Redaction is not evenly distributed — companies redact at 69.7% and financial institutions at 70.8%, against 22.7% for academia and 20.0% for data/analytics providers.

And redacted respondents are less supportive of almost every proposal: hourly matching 1.81 against 2.37 for named respondents, fossil fallback 2.76 against 3.41, residual-mix definition 3.30 against 3.80, feasibility-measure sufficiency 2.01 against 2.66. The legacy clause is the single reversal (4.63 redacted against 4.51 named).

The consequence is structural, not incidental: any headline built only on attributable responses skews supportive — and the organisation browser, the quote attributions and the entire legitimacy audit are named-only by construction. Read them knowing they over-represent the more supportive half. country, organization_type and sector are populated for all 1,072, so those remain the reliable segmentation dimensions.

Response rates vary enormously — always divide by n

Questions range from 1,072 answers (the profile questions) down to 75 (Q13, Q110). Within the MBM anchors alone: Q71 and Q83 drew ~900 answers, Q97 and Q124 drew 522. The median respondent answered 46 of 166 substantive questions; 88 answered more than 75% of them.

So a raw count of option picks conflates "most people disagree" with "most people skipped it", and no percentage on this site is shown without its base. Multi-select percentages sum to more than 100% by design, and per-respondent booleans are built from response_selections rather than option totals.

A trap worth naming. SQLite columns in this database are TEXT-typed, so numeric comparisons must cast explicitly. WHERE substantive_completion_pct > 75 compares strings and returns 120; WHERE substantive_completion_pct+0.0 > 75 returns 88. The root README.md's "only 120 respondents answered more than 75%" and "43 answered under 10%" are the uncast comparisons — numerically the figures are 88 and 173. This page uses the cast values. Write +0 or +0.0 into every comparison and aggregate.

Organisation names are uncontrolled free text

Q4 and Q5 are free-text fields, so organization is not a controlled vocabulary. Across 499 named respondents there are 494 distinct strings; one is the placeholder "Not provided", and the longest runs 3,890 characters of pasted prose rather than a name. The same body appears under different spellings, in different scripts, with and without legal suffixes.

Practical consequences: truncate organisation labels before displaying them, and join on respondent_id only — never on name. The entity families in section 4 are a curated patch over the worst of this, not a resolution of it, and they will miss any group whose spellings nobody thought to pattern-match.

Truncated answers and paste artefacts

The survey tool capped free text at 4,000 characters. Eight answers hit the cap and carry likely_truncated = 1; their argument may simply stop mid-sentence, so a missing conclusion in one of those answers is a limit of the instrument and not a position.

Around 1,900 exotic space characters — non-breaking, narrow-no-break, thin, ideographic, zero-width — arrived with answers pasted out of Word and are normalised at build time. Two Q118 answers arrived as JSON-ish literals (["4 - Mostly ready"]) and are normalised to the plain label. Q140 and Q174 mix write-ins into their option lists and are flagged review_option_split = 1: filter is_canonical = 1, and re-read question_options if you disagree with where the line was drawn. Q67's top two options overlap as worded, so their split is not a clean preference.

Q183: protest years

Q183 asks for a uniform effective date as a free-text year, not a picklist (n=207). Seventeen answers fall above 2050, including eleven 2099s — protest or sentinel values, not forecasts. They are flagged rather than dropped, and never averaged in blindly: including them moves the mean by years.

The question is also framed counterfactually — "if a uniform effective date was applied rather than a legacy clause" — so an answer to it does not imply the respondent prefers that approach. Reading the Q183 distribution as support for a uniform date inverts the meaning for a large share of respondents, most of whom supported the legacy clause instead (Q171 mean 4.58, the tightest consensus in the survey).

What the legitimacy audit cannot claim. The classification audit and the coordination analysis are the sharpest instruments on this site, and their limits need stating as precisely as their findings.
  • Redaction is permitted, and unverifiable is not a verdict. Requesting redaction was an option the consultation offered. unverifiable means an identity was withheld so a claim cannot be checked — it does not mean illegitimate, and no redacted respondent is named, guessed at, or inferred anywhere on this site.
  • Coordinated and template submissions are lawful consultation behaviour, and they occur on all sides. Shared text shows shared drafting. It is evidence about independence — a shared citation is not corroboration — and not evidence of wrongdoing.
  • Classification is not accusation. Recording that a body is a trade association, a think tank or a data vendor describes what it is; it says nothing about whether its argument is right. For the same reason a certification body and an industry group are never summed into one headline: reclassifications into business-interest classes and reclassifications into finer non-profit classes are reported as two separate numbers.
  • It cannot measure motive, funding, or correctness. Interest and accuracy are independent — a respondent with a commercial stake can be right, and the data-vendor and corporate fingerprints are presented symmetrically for that reason. The audit reads names, self-descriptions and write-ins; it does not read intentions.
  • It carries a confidence field because some calls are judgement. low means exactly that. No web research was used, so a well-known body is classified from common knowledge and an obscure one is left unverifiable rather than resolved.
  • Small-n caveats apply throughout. Several organisation types have single-digit bases on the later anchors (financial institutions n=7–8 on Q97–Q124; government n=8–14), and any segment under 5 respondents is suppressed entirely rather than shown.

6. Reproduce it

Six commands take you from the published spreadsheet to this site. Every one below was run against this commit; the output quoted is what it printed.

Step 1 — rebuild the tidy dataset from the source workbook

pip install openpyxl
python3 scripts/build_dataset.py
Ran in ~22 s. The only step that needs a dependency — openpyxl, to read the .xlsx. Deterministic: the same input gives byte-identical output, which is why re-running it is safe. One wrinkle worth knowing: re-running rewrites data/scope2_consultation.xlsx with a fresh zip timestamp, so git status reports that one file as modified even though its contents are unchanged — git checkout -- data/scope2_consultation.xlsx resets it.
