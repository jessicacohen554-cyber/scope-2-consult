Standard Supply: Feasibility & Opposition

In brief

  • Contested, not rejected. Of 522 who answered Q97, 254 support SSS (48.7%), 110 are neutral and 158 oppose (30.3%). Mean 3.14 of 5.
  • Two different oppositions. One is about practicality and comes from all sides: definitions, data, regional fit. The other is about principle and comes almost entirely from opponents. 71.8% of those who ticked a principle objection in Q100 oppose SSS. Of those who ticked only practical concerns, 53.5% support it.
  • Definition and data top the free text. Of 470 respondents with substantive SSS comments, 50.0% say the scope is unclear: FIT, CfD, partial subsidies, public ownership. 44.9% say supplier data or registries do not exist. Supporters raise both as often as opponents do.
  • Opposition is geographic. Japan (43 of 60 oppose) and Korea (10 of 11) supply 53 of 158 opponents (33.5%) from 13.6% of answerers. Their argument is that SSS would reclassify certificates from publicly supported generation that their national systems already let companies buy and claim.
  • The proposed implementers are the least supportive. 55.3% of energy suppliers/utilities and 6 of 9 registry operators oppose. Utilities most often cite unclear scope (33 of 45 commenting) and lost voluntary demand (24).
  • Not a proxy vote on the package. 106 of 309 hourly-matching opponents (34.3%) support SSS. SSS draws support across the camps that split on hourly matching.
  • The registry gap has no consensus fix. On a default designation where data are missing (Q107): 141 yes, 110 no, 89 unsure. Proposed remedies are scattered: region-specific guidance (85), registry first (73), self-estimated share (66), disclosure (65), phase-in (60), asset-age rule (49).

Assessment: what each challenge needs

Our reading of the coded comments. Who can resolve each challenge: the standard’s drafters, outside infrastructure, or a policy choice that some stakeholders will lose.

ChallengeResolved byAssessment
Unclear scope
235 respondents		Drafting The most-raised issue. Supporters raise it as often as opponents. Commenters ask for an operational test: what is a mandatory financial relationship, how partial subsidies, CfDs, FIPs and publicly owned legacy hydro or nuclear classify, and a worked list by jurisdiction. The Protocol can fix this in the text, but every classification choice it makes moves some market into or out of SSS.
No supplier data or registry
211		Infrastructure Outside the Protocol’s control. Suppliers, regulators or registries must publish pro-rata allocations. US respondents describe utilities publishing fleet-average rates, not delivered-supply mixes. Until that changes, a reporter cannot apply SSS consistently. Q107 shows no agreement on a default: a default restores coverage but invites the inconsistency opponents fear.
Uneven across regions
172		Infrastructure + Drafting A consequence of the data gap. Two firms with the same load can report different market-based totals depending on whether their region can allocate SSS. Disclosure rules (data source, SSS line item, fallback used) reduce this but do not remove it.
Does not fit our market
133		Policy choice The hardest conflict. In Japan, FIT non-fossil certificates are sold to companies and the revenue reduces the surcharge on all users. Respondents argue the buyer pays extra, so the claim is not double-counted. Korea, China and Hong Kong raise similar points. Treating these as SSS would remove the basis for existing “100% renewable” products. Exempting them weakens the principle that pro-rata shares belong to all payers. No wording satisfies both.
Overlap with EACs / residual mix
96		Drafting Raised more by supporters (41) than opponents (22). It is mostly a request to define the order of operations, so that SSS, voluntary EACs and the residual mix do not count the same MWh twice. Solvable alongside the residual-mix definition.
Who pays ≠ who claims
61		Drafting + Policy Levy exemptions for energy-intensive users, tax-funded schemes and customer-class tariffs mean “pro rata” has no single basis. Choosing one basis (load, bill share or customer class) is a drafting task. Any choice favours some payers over others.
Crowds out voluntary demand
98		Policy choice The core principled objection, concentrated among utilities and in Japan. It is partly empirical: does shrinking the claimable pool raise prices for new projects, or suppress demand? The consultation offers assertion, not evidence, on either side.

How this was measured
  • Stance: Q97, 1–5. Support = 4–5, oppose = 1–2.
  • Corpus: every substantive answer to the ten SSS free-text questions (Q99, Q101–103, Q105–106, Q108, Q110–112), plus ±400 characters around any mention of SSS or standard supply elsewhere. 484 respondents, 470 with substantive content.
  • Coding: AI coders read each respondent’s text against a fixed codebook of 8 feasibility challenges, 5 objections in principle and 8 remedies, plus a text stance. Respondents with identical SSS text were harmonised to one code by majority (39 cells changed). Every quote was checked to be verbatim. A random sample was checked by hand. No second-coder agreement score.
  • Shared wording: 73 respondents filed SSS answers that open with text shared by at least two others. They are counted once each, as filed.
  • Limits: SSS questions were optional, and about half of all respondents skipped them. Coding records what was said, not how strongly. The assessment table is the analyst’s judgement.
