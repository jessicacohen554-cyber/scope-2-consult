Cohort = 35 institutions that self-classified as a financial institution, were audited as one, or are finance- or insurance-sector companies, plus 14 investor networks and banking or insurance associations. Rule and exclusions in the method note at the foot of the page.

In brief

  • One label, three voices. The cohort's average on hourly matching (2.17 of 5, 8 of 35 in support) looks like the whole survey (2.07), but it averages opposites. Financial firms reporting their own office and branch footprint oppose it 11 of 11, and capital providers and project financiers 6 of 7. The few investors who use companies' disclosures and answered lean the other way (2 of 3), as do investor networks (2 of 3). Small numbers; the direction, not the ratio, is the finding.
  • The one thing every role backs is conservatism. The residual-mix definition (14 of 14 support, mean 4.57 against 3.54 overall), the fossil-based fallback (11 of 12, 4.50 against 3.07) and pro-rata standard supply (10 of 15, 3.80 against 3.14) draw support from users and reporters alike. Investors say it in their own words: errors should fall "on the conservative side".
  • Additionality is raised by a quarter, by every role except reporters. 12 of 48 distinct voices say the proposal should recognise whether procurement caused new supply; none argue the opposite. They split on where: seven want it inside the inventory, by differentiating instruments or adding a vintage or standard-supply test (PGIM, Nordea, First Sentier, Just Share, a redacted Canadian banking body); four want a separate capital-impact channel beside a conservative inventory (Ceres and three redacted finance filers); Just Climate asks for both.
  • Openness to a separate impact metric is well above the survey. Half of the cohort's Q146 answers say such a metric would change their view at least somewhat (30% overall) and only 10% reject the idea (24% overall). The disagreement is about standing: capital providers want it on "equal footing" with the inventory; investors using disclosures want it separate and explicitly not a reason to loosen inventory rules.
  • The legacy clause is supported by 29 of 33, but the design asks point in opposite directions. Banking and insurance bodies, reporters and some financiers want full protection: the whole original term, every instrument type, transfers, renewals, even asset-level grandfathering. Investors using disclosures and investor networks want guardrails: a sunset, a minimum original term, no unbundled certificates, no resale, and side-by-side results with and without legacy treatment. Three of the six investors using disclosures prefer a uniform effective date or parallel reporting instead.
  • Bankability is the finance-specific argument, disclosure the finance-specific ask. 20 voices say hourly matching and deliverability would fracture load aggregation, PPA volumes and project finance; none of them are investors using disclosures. 11 ask that whatever flexibility survives (exemptions, legacy, load profiles, standard supply) be disclosed and disaggregated so users can see it; most of those are investors using disclosures or investor networks.
  • Coordination shows here too. One redacted UK filing repeats PGIM's submission in 107 sentences and is counted once. Just Share's text is the pro-hourly template (57 other filers share it); two redacted US finance firms carry sentences from the opposing packs; and three banking bodies file near-identical legacy-clause paragraphs.

How this was measured
  • Cohort: institutions = self-classified "Financial Institution" (24), or audited as financial in reference/org_audit.csv (adds 4), or a finance- or insurance-sector company not audited as an individual (adds 6), plus one redacted asset-management company (35 in all). Associations = 14 hand-listed investor networks and banking or insurance bodies. Excluded: an independent advisor, a GRESB contractor, two data vendors in the finance sector, an environmental-commodity trader filing as a consultant, and real-estate investment associations.
  • Role: coded from how the respondent describes itself in its text (investor using disclosures; capital provider or project financier; reporter of its own footprint; investor network; banking or insurance association; market intermediary; not stated). Never inferred from the organisation name.
  • Themes and legacy design: every submission (561,000 characters of free text) read in full by one AI coder; 14 themes, an impact-metric stance and a legacy-design code per respondent, each with a verbatim quote that the exporter checks against the database. Conservative: a theme is coded only when stated. No second-coder agreement statistic.
  • Scale answers: Q71, 83, 97, 113, 124, 153, 171 (1–5 support scales; 4–5 = support). Cohort bases are small (13–36 per question) and are shown on every chart.
  • Duplication: respondent 130 (redacted UK individual) shares 107 sentences with PGIM (598) and is excluded from theme, legacy and impact tallies. Sentence sharing with non-cohort filers is flagged per respondent.
  • Names: redacted and individual filers are described by claimed type, sector and country, never named, even where their text identifies them.
