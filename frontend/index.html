What happened here

The GHG Protocol's consultation on revising the Scope 2 market-based method drew 1,072 respondents — 915 answering for organizations, 157 as individuals — who left 70,089 answers across 180 questions. Participation was uneven: the median respondent answered between a quarter and half of the questionnaire, and only 88 worked through more than three-quarters of it. More consequentially, 573 respondents — 53.5% — filed with their identity redacted, and redaction is not evenly spread: roughly 70% of companies and of financial institutions withheld their names, against 23% of academic respondents and 20% of data-and-analytics vendors.

One caution belongs ahead of every number below: a consultation is not a referendum. Respondents selected themselves, both camps organized filings, and who shows up attributably differs from who shows up at all. The counts on this site measure mobilization and stated positions, not where the truth lies — the methodology page states these limits in full.

The headline shape

Seven questions asked directly whether respondents support each market-based-method proposal, and together they are the consultation's spine — the chart above, explorable by segment in the Likert Explorer. The two proposals that would impose new requirements were rejected decisively: hourly matching drew 70.2% opposition (mean 2.07 of 5, n=909) and deliverability requirements 59.0% (2.40, n=875). The three design questions in the middle split respondents: pro-rata standard-supply allocation at 3.14, the residual-mix definition at 3.54, the fossil-based fallback at 3.07. And the two flexibility proposals were embraced: exemptions from hourly matching at 76.3% support (4.06, n=748) and the legacy clause — grandfathering existing contracts — at 90.0% (4.58, n=801), the tightest consensus in the survey.

The same shape repeats in the design details: 67.1% picked the largest exemption threshold offered (50 GWh); 57.3% want exemptions ongoing rather than time-limited; 71.7% would extend legacy eligibility to every contract signed before implementation; and 83.4% chose the legacy clause over a uniform effective date as the transition path.

Who supports, who opposes — and why it maps to interest

Split the anchors by organization type and the pattern tracks commercial position closely. Companies (mean 1.65 on hourly matching, n=429) and industry groups (1.68, n=76) anchor the opposition; NGOs (3.07) and academics (2.97, n=31) sit near the middle; and the only organization type whose average supports hourly matching is data-and-analytics providers (4.10, n=21) — the firms that would sell the metering, matching and certificate tooling a requirement creates demand for. The mirror image completes the fingerprint: those same vendors are the coolest of any type toward the exemptions (3.56) and legacy clause (3.81) that would let reporters avoid buying, while companies back the legacy clause at 4.71.

The interest on the opposing side is equally legible — and candidly stated. 83.5% of legacy-clause supporters picked the reason citing the “significant financial and operational commitments” embedded in existing contracts: corporates protecting the value of RECs and PPAs already on their books. Both readings are facts about incentive, not correctness — a vendor supporting a requirement it would profit from and a reporter opposing a requirement it would pay for are the same kind of fact, and neither settles whether the proposal is good accounting. Small numbers deserve an explicit hedge: financial institutions (n=7–19 per anchor) and governments (n=8–14) move on a handful of answers, and the vendor column itself rests on 16–21.

Scientific integrity, weighed

Supporters of hourly matching look better-researched on exactly one behavior: citing. 34.3% of supporters show a hard citation signal (URL, DOI, journal reference) against 14.7% of opponents — a clean 2.3× separation. That advantage does not extend to what respondents said their own judgment rests on. Asked directly, 7.6% of opponents and 8.0% of supporters claimed direct empirical analysis — statistically the same — and supporters were somewhat more likely to claim operational experience with hourly accounting (29.3% vs 21.6%). The tempting narrative that opponents bring more first-hand operational evidence is not supported by this data; neither is the narrative that supporters have done more measuring.

Two discounts apply to the citation edge. Part of it is circulated rather than independent: 17 citation-bearing passages recur verbatim across 114 submissions — 85% of that traffic is copies. And the sources lean partisan: advocacy-classified domains (energytag.org, resource-solutions.org, theclimategroup.org) draw more linked references in aggregate (344) than strictly peer-reviewed hosts (261), with preprint servers (185) between them. The opposition's case rests on a different kind of evidence — self-report about themselves. Reporters say hourly matching would push internal effort (4.74 of 5) and external cost (4.73) far above today's, expect procurement prices to rise (4.66), rate registry infrastructure unready (2.21 of 5) and the proposed feasibility measures insufficient (2.33). That is self-interested and unverifiable from outside — and also consistent, first-hand, and about the one subject on which reporters are the best-placed witnesses. The honest verdict: the support side cites more external research; both sides claim similar first-hand basis; only 27 respondents anywhere (7.4% of those asked) did direct empirical analysis; and neither side is disinterested.

Concerns that survive scrutiny

Strip away motive and several opposition arguments still stand on their own facts. Infrastructure: 44% of those asked could not estimate how long residual-mix registries would take to stand up, and only 2.3% grounded their readiness view in pilot or production use. Regional equity: 80.2% of fossil-fallback opponents flagged disproportionate impact on organizations in regions without residual-mix data — a concern supporters' own comments echo. Market participation: 66.2% of deliverability opponents warned of a shift away from long-term PPAs toward spot purchases. Comparability: the top concern among exemption opponents (50.9%) is inconsistency across companies. And definitional vagueness: 73.3% of standard-supply opponents chose “unclear rules/definition”.

Support arguments that survive scrutiny

The same test leaves real arguments standing on the other side. Annual matching lets a reporter claim solar generation against overnight consumption — the misallocation critique (“solar at night,” selected by 74.3% of hourly-matching supporters) that is grounded in a peer-reviewed literature both camps cite. 65.9% flagged greenwashing and time-shifting of claims; 57.4% chose the price-signal argument — that hourly claims steer money toward the hours and places where clean power is actually scarce. That literature is genuinely contested — one academic filing cites the empirical work on both sides of the hourly debate — but the integrity problem it identifies is real accounting substance, not something manufactured by vendors.

Coordination and classification

Both camps ran coordinated campaigns. At the supportive end, 22 filings share a byte-identical answer pattern across all seven anchors plus 48 verbatim passages — the pro-hourly-matching bloc, whose named members include EnergyTag, TenneT, Fervo Energy, The Breakthrough Institute, Bellona Europa, As You Sow, SIDENOR and Princeton's ZERO lab, alongside five redacted filings. What could read as independent academic corroboration of the vendor position is, in part, one shared response pack. At the opposing end: one electronics group filed twelve times, and SEMI and member companies share 46 verbatim texts while the Avary/FPC supply-chain family filed six identical answer patterns. A third campaign fits neither camp cleanly: the Euro Certificate bloc — 15 named European certificate-market organizations including RECS, Eurelectric, Fortum and Landsvirkjun — shares 24 passages defending the annual certificate market it operates in: against pro-rata standard-supply limits, for the legacy clause.

Self-classification is unreliable in both directions: of 66 named respondents claiming “NGO/civil society,” the audit moved 22 into business-interest classes (trade associations, business coalitions, a vendor); 9 of 34 “academia/research” claims are not academic institutions. And because redacted respondents are systematically less supportive — the redacted mean is lower on six of the seven anchors, with the legacy clause the sole reversal — any headline built only on attributable filings skews supportive.

How an objective third party should read this

Read the counts as a map of who mobilized, not a vote on what is true. That said, the headline direction is robust to the duplication this site can measure: collapsing every named entity family and bloc to a single vote moves named opposition to hourly matching only from 62.7% to 62.4%. Trust most the places where positions cross interest lines: the residual-mix definition (3.54) draws support from both camps, consultants (2.46) and academics (2.97) sit mid-scale on hourly matching, and the few government respondents split — opposing hourly matching (2.07, n=14) while supporting deliverability (4.09, n=11). What the consultation cannot settle is the empirical dispute at its center: whether hourly matching's accuracy gains justify its costs. The 27 respondents who did direct analysis split roughly like everyone else (73% opposed) — published back-testing and registry pilots would say more than another 1,072 filings.

Nuance and coalitions

Neither camp is a monolith. The pro-hourly bloc spans software vendors, a steelmaker, a clean-firm generator, NGOs and university labs; the opposition spans Japanese utilities (FEPC and TEPCO filing in parallel), a semiconductor supply chain organized through SEMI, industrial-gas associations (EIGA/SIGA), and European certificate incumbents whose businesses depend on the market-based method itself surviving. Positions cross-cut: financial institutions oppose hourly matching (2.32) yet are the strongest supporters of the fossil fallback (4.86 — on seven answers); NGOs sit close to neutral on the hourly requirement (3.07) while backing the exemptions from it (4.24). And the consultation's one near-universal position is the one that changes existing claims least: 90% support for the legacy clause necessarily includes most hourly-matching supporters too.
