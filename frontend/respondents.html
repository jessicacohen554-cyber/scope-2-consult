Read this page first. A consultation is not a poll: respondents self-select, and organized campaigns can move counts. Before any stance on this site means anything, it matters who filed, how much of the survey they answered, and whether they attached a name to it — because none of those three are evenly distributed.

Organization type, and who signed their name

Every respondent picked one of 15 organization types. Redaction is not spread evenly across them — it tracks commercial exposure almost perfectly.

Respondents by organization type, named vs redacted

The redaction gradient

Corporates redact; analysts and academics don't. Roughly seven in ten companies and financial institutions withheld their organization name, against 20–28% of data/analytics providers, academic and research bodies, and NGOs. The pattern fits who carries a commercial position: firms that must report under this guidance filed without a name, while firms and institutions that comment on reporting — including the vendors who would sell hourly-matching tooling — put their names to it. That gradient is the reason the named-org browser lower down is not a representative sample of who filed.

Named vs redacted mean, per anchor

Only the seven support anchors appear here. The other 15 scale questions use different constructs — on the burden and cost rows 3 means "same as today" and 5 is a complaint, not support — and pooling them under one ramp would misread them. They are on the Likert Explorer.

Headlines built on named responses skew supportive. On six of the seven anchors the respondents who withheld their name are less supportive than the ones who did not, by up to 0.65 of a scale point (Q124 fossil fallback: 3.41 named against 2.76 redacted). Quote them, attribute them, build a story out of the filings you can cite by name — and you will overstate support for every requirement in the package. The lone exception is Q171, the legacy clause, where redacted respondents are more supportive (4.63 against 4.51): protection for contracts already signed is the one thing the anonymous majority wanted more than the named minority.

How to read this page

Organization is uncontrolled free text. The consultation form asked for an organization name and accepted whatever was typed: real names, placeholders ("ABC", "None", "Not provided"), job titles, and in a few cases several paragraphs of prose describing the filer. Names in the browser above are truncated to 80 characters and are shown exactly as submitted, so some rows read like sentences. Nothing joins on the name — every lookup keys on the respondent id — and a name that looks like a placeholder has not been corrected or guessed at.

Redacted is not illegitimate. 573 respondents did not attach an organization name. They answered the same questions and their answers carry the same weight in every count and mean on this site; they simply cannot be attributed, audited, or quoted by name, and this site never names them. Anonymity here is a structural feature of the response base, not a verdict about a respondent — the honest reading is that attributable and withheld responses are two populations with measurably different stances, and the withheld one is larger.

Self-declared fields are self-declared. Organization type, sector, and country are the respondent's own answers, not verified attributes. The "audited class" column in the browser is our reading of what a named organization actually is, with the basis stated inside each profile; where the two disagree, both are shown. Redacted respondents have no audited class because there is nothing to check.

Free text was capped. Answers were recorded up to 4,000 characters. Eight answers land exactly on that limit and are marked likely truncated where they appear in a profile — the submitted text continued past what was stored.

Definitions, derivation of the audit classes and template flags, and the full caveat list are on the methodology page.
